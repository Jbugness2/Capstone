Personal Statement Essay

Arriving at the edge of safe ground, my body began to freeze. I knew the only way to safety was to push forward. As cliffs fell on all sides, I realized how little I knew.

My brother and I had just gotten back from a two-day, 24-mile backpacking trip when our dad invited us to climb a fourteener with him and our family friends. Ecstatically, we responded, “Of course, when are we leaving?”

“In two days.”

In my ignorance, I assumed a day was a reasonable amount of time to recover, both mentally and physically. Two days later, we arrived at the trailhead, leaving behind a parking lot full of cars. The trail itself was completely empty. I didn’t think this was strange because I knew there were two peaks. So I assumed everyone was hiking the second peak — oblivious to this warning, I continued forward.

As we kept going, the trail slowly dissipated until a rocky ridge was our only path. As we journeyed forward, orientation became much harder, with only scarce cairns left by previous climbers to help us find our way. The higher we climbed, the more I began to shake. I grew up with a climbing wall in my house, stretching from our basement to our attic, three stories high. Heights weren’t new to me, but like most I still feel fear. I looked down at what seemed like a thousand-foot drop, and my legs wouldn’t stop trembling.

My dad glanced back. “Are you doing okay?”

Being the only one fazed by such a drop, I of course answered, “Yeah, I’m fine.” With a slightly high pitched voice as tremors coursed through my body.

The ridge narrowed until I was crawling along its sharp spine. I began to wonder how long it would take to reach the bottom. Being truly brilliant, I came to the conclusion that I didn’t want to find out, so I continued forward with the group. On the far side, we ran into a wall of rock. To the left, a fall that would have been fatal at half its height. To the right, a steep slope with a narrow ledge pressed against the stone, the bottom of the slope couldn’t be seen due to the cliff at the end of it.

Halfway across the ledge, I saw what came next: nearly twenty feet of slick stone we needed to stem up, with nowhere else to go. I climbed. One mistake, one loose rock, even a small lapse in focus could cost me everything. I reached the top drenched in sweat, and not from the effort. When we finally reached the summit, I was stunned. The view was incredible, but what caught my attention wasn’t the sky. It was the wide and safe trail, running from the parking lot all the way to the summit. Everyone else had taken the walking path up, and I hadn’t even known there was a choice. I was relieved to take the easy path down.

I still think about that. I didn’t lack courage that day. I lacked information. Nobody misguided me; I just never asked.

It took the safe way down for me to understand where my brilliance actually comes from. It clearly isn’t planning. It’s adapting. I had no idea what I was walking into, and I climbed it anyway. But adapting is a strength not a strategy. What I truly learned from this experience is to ask questions in all situations. I want to learn more so I can plan more and adapt better when plans change.

So I strive to ask first, and I make sure the people around me know where the trail leads.
